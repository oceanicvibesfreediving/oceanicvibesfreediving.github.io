## Beyond the Course: How to Find a Freediving Teacher Who Adapts to You

This week I read a question from a freediver who had finished their AIDA 3 and wanted more — not another course, but a teacher who would sit with them and see what was actually wrong. They didn't know how to find that person outside the school system. It is a good question, and a familiar one. Most of us hit our first plateau right after certification.

## What a course can't see

A certification course is built to bring many people to the same standard in a few days. That is its job, and it does it well. But a course teaches the minimum you need to be safe. It cannot spend an afternoon watching how you breathe before a dive, or notice that you clench your jaw at fifteen meters, or learn that your static always stalls at the same moment because of something that happened on your first deep dive.

That is where a teacher who adapts to you matters. They can see what a curriculum cannot: your tension, your habits, the reason your depth or static has stopped moving. Often the reason is not technique. It is patience. Static gains come from spaced max attempts, consistent CO2 work, and relaxation — not from chasing a new personal best every session. A coach who adapts knows when to tell you to stop testing and just breathe.

## What to look for

The name on the certificate matters less than how the person teaches. Watch them before you book. Do they watch you before they talk? Do they ask what you are afraid of, not just what you want to achieve? Do they adjust the plan to your body, or do they have a script?

The biggest sign is safety culture. A good coach has a visible one: a buddy who is actually watching, clear hand signals, an honest read of conditions, and the willingness to say "not today." Anyone who romanticizes risk, or pushes you past your comfort to prove something, is not adapting to you. They are using you.

Honesty is the other test. A coach who adapts will tell you when you are not ready, when your gear is wrong, when a goal is too soon. They will also admit what they do not know. That honesty is what earns trust, and trust is what lets you relax. Relaxation is where depth actually comes from.

## Questions to ask before you book

Ask direct questions. "What do you see in my breathing?" "How do you handle a student who is afraid?" "What is your safety protocol on a deep session?" "What do we do when I do not improve?" A good answer is specific. A vague answer is a warning.

Ask about their own diving, too. Not their deepest number — their relationship with the water. The best coaches are the ones who still describe themselves as students. They have stopped performing calm and learned to trust the current. That is the person you want holding your line.

## Where to find them

The best teachers are rarely found through search results. Ask your instructor if they know someone who coaches outside the school framework. Ask in the local freediving community. In Playa del Carmen and Bacalar, the community is small; the person known for patience travels by word of mouth, not by advertising.

When you find them, expect a first session that is mostly talking and watching. That is a good sign. A teacher who adapts to you starts by learning you.

The seam between control and surrender is different for every diver. A course can show you where the seam is. A teacher who adapts to you helps you decide when to cross it — and when to come up.
