This week a student sent me a month of static apnea logs. Every session was a max attempt. Every session ended within two seconds of the one before. Her note said: "I think I've hit my ceiling."

She hadn't. She'd been testing the same wall every day and calling it training.

If you've just finished a beginner course, you know the feeling. The first weeks are a straight line up — ten seconds here, twenty there. Then the line flattens. The stopwatch stops moving. It's easy to read that as a verdict: *I'm not built for this.*

It's not a verdict. It's the adaptation curve doing its normal work.

## The wall you're hitting isn't a ceiling

That fast start is real, but most of it is neurological. Your body is learning that holding your breath is safe — that the urge to breathe isn't an emergency. Relaxation improves, technique clicks, and the numbers climb.

Then the easy gains are gone, and the slower work begins: blood volume, spleen response, a diaphragm that can stay still. That work takes weeks, not sessions. The plateau is the body consolidating. It's not a stop — it's where the adaptation actually happens.

How do you tell a plateau from a problem? How you feel between sessions. A normal plateau is calm — you're not improving, but you're not sliding backward. If your maxes are dropping, your sleep is restless, or your chest feels tight all day, that's not a plateau. That's overtraining, and the fix is rest, not more tables.

## You can't test your way to a new max

Here's the uncomfortable truth: chasing a personal best every session trains the wrong thing. A max attempt is stress. Do it daily and your nervous system starts associating the breath hold with effort and a little fear. That's the opposite of what freediving needs.

The skill you're actually training is relaxation under pressure. The stopwatch is just output. It measures the result, not the work.

So the honest answer to how to improve your breath hold is almost boring:

- **Space your max attempts.** Once a week, maybe every two. Test your max — don't train it.
- **Do CO2 tables.** Short breath-holds with a fixed, slowly shrinking recovery. They're repetitive and they work: they raise your tolerance for the urge to breathe, which is the thing that actually ends a breath hold. Do them dry, with a timer and a buddy who knows what they're watching.
- **Practice relaxation.** A long static is mostly a meditation with a timer. Learn to let your shoulders drop, your jaw go soft, your thoughts go quiet.
- **Rest honestly.** Adaptation happens between sessions, not during them. A tired body defends its numbers. A rested one gives them up.

## What a training trip should be for

This is where it gets personal, because it's about where you're going.

If you've booked a week in Playa del Carmen or Bacalar, the temptation is to treat it as seven chances to set a new best. Don't. A short window is exactly when you should stop chasing numbers.

The water there rewards comfort, not effort. The warm, buoyant salt of the Riviera Maya carries you. Bacalar's freshwater lies flat and clear, and diving it well means slowing down. A week of relaxed, skill-focused work — equalization, finning, comfort at depth — will move your breath hold more than seven max attempts ever will. And it's safer. A tired diver pushing for a number on day four is how bad decisions happen. That's a risk not worth taking, anywhere.

Dive the reef off Playa without touching it. Treat the cenotes as what they are — living, sacred places. That's just how divers live here. And never dive alone. The buddy system is the sport's first rule, and its best one.

## Patience is the skill

In a cenote you can see the halocline — the seam where salt water meets fresh, sharp as a line drawn in the water. The seam you're trying to cross was never that one. It's the distance between the breath you hold for yourself and the one that brings you up. That distance closes with patience, not pressure — and patience is trainable, like any other skill.

If you're planning a training week, build it around one goal: to be more comfortable in the water than when you arrived.
